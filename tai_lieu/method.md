**Field data acquisition.** Each trunk was scanned with a LiDAR-equipped iPhone (12 Pro or later) using a modified build of the Stray Scanner application, which records synchronised RGB video (1920 × 1440 pixels), LiDAR depth maps, a per-frame camera pose from ARKit visual–inertial odometry, inertial measurements and GPS position. Consumer-grade LiDAR on these devices has been validated for forest inventory (Gollob et al., 2021) and for broader geoscientific applications (Luetzenburg et al., 2021). The operator walked around the trunk while sweeping the device vertically, so that every face of the trunk was filmed from several angles and heights.

**3D reconstruction.** Depth and colour frames (every fifth frame) were back-projected into a coloured point cloud in the ARKit world frame, whose vertical axis is aligned with gravity, and down-sampled on a 3 mm voxel grid. The target trunk was located as the least-squares convergence point of the cameras' horizontal viewing rays, since the operator keeps the target in frame throughout the scan; point density was not used for this, because it was unreliable when neighbouring trees were in view. Ground returns were removed by RANSAC plane fitting (Fischler and Bolles, 1981), and the trunk was then traced upward in thin height slices, each with its own centre, which accommodates lean and taper. Green vegetation was removed by hue and saturation (hue 65–150°, saturation > 0.25), off-axis fragments by clustering within each slice, and the remaining points were reduced to the largest DBSCAN cluster (Ester et al., 1996). A surface mesh was reconstructed from the trunk point cloud by Poisson surface reconstruction (Kazhdan et al., 2006) at octree depth 8 and trimmed at the 2nd percentile of reconstruction density. Because Poisson reconstruction always closes a surface, 6 cm height slices whose cross-section contained an angular gap wider than 20° — trunk the camera never saw — were removed beforehand, so that unobserved bark was not added to the measured area. Mesh vertices more than 5 cm from any trunk point were then discarded, and the mesh was smoothed with 20 iterations of Taubin smoothing (Taubin, 1995).

**Lichen detection.** In this material lichen and bark share the same hue, and a trunk lit from one side varies in brightness far more than lichen differs from bark. Colour was therefore aggregated per point from the original video frames rather than read from the fused point cloud. For every second frame, points were projected into the image using the recorded pose and camera intrinsics; occluded points were removed with a depth buffer on 4-pixel cells, and a point was sampled only where its surface faced the camera (angle between surface normal and viewing ray below 69.5°). Frames whose trunk region was blurred (variance of the Laplacian below 60) were discarded. Colour was converted to CIELAB and averaged over all remaining observations, and points observed in fewer than five frames were excluded. Six features were computed per point. The first two are the a\* and b\* values relative to the median of all points whose surface normals face the same direction (16 sectors of 22.5° around the trunk), which removes the directional component of illumination. The other four are the L\* and a\* values relative to the median of the 300 and of the 1,000 nearest neighbours, restricted to neighbours with near-parallel normals (within 25.8°), which measures local contrast within a single face of the trunk. Lightness enters only as this local contrast: absolute lightness, and lightness relative to the same side of the trunk, were excluded because they followed illumination more closely than lichen and lowered cross-validated accuracy. A logistic-regression classifier on these features was trained on 165,887 points hand-labelled from photographs of 10 trees. Under leave-one-tree-out cross-validation, in which each tree is scored by a model that never saw it, the classifier reached a mean F1 score of 40.6%, against 13.0% for the HSV local-contrast detector used previously.

**Coverage quantification.** Point labels were transferred to mesh vertices by majority vote of the 15 nearest points, and to each triangle by majority of its three vertices. Triangles on the flat end caps, and within 5% of the trunk length from either end, were counted as bark. Lichen area was taken as the summed area of lichen-labelled triangles. The per-tree coverage fraction produced by the classifier was, however, not correlated with the hand-labelled coverage of the same trees (Pearson r = 0.07, n = 10), and assigning the corpus mean to every tree was more accurate (mean absolute error 8.8 versus 19.0 percentage points). The classifier therefore locates lichen on a trunk but does not measure how much is present, and per-tree coverage values were not used. Coverage was instead estimated for the sampled population directly from the hand-labelled trees: 23.2% (95% confidence interval 14.4–32.0%, n = 10). Trees were selected in the field partly because lichen was visible on their sunlit side, so this figure describes the scanned sample rather than the plantation as a whole.

**Orientation.** The distribution of lichen by trunk aspect was not analysed. The raw data contain no measurement of terrain slope; the only direction available is the lean of the trunk itself, which is small (0.1–10.3°) and, on most trees, below the threshold at which a split direction can be defined at all. Because trees were also selected according to the side on which lichen was visible, this sample could not show which aspect lichen favours.
